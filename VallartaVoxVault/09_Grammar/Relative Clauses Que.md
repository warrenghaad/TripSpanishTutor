# Relative Clauses (que)

Spanish leans heavily on *que* to link clauses. Master *que* and your sentences get longer without getting harder.

## que — the workhorse

Used for both people and things.

- *El hotel **que** reservé está cerca.* — The hotel that I booked is nearby.
- *La mujer **que** habla español me ayudó.* — The woman who speaks Spanish helped me.
- *El platillo **que** recomendó estuvo delicioso.* — The dish (that) you recommended was delicious.

English often drops "that" / "who" / "which." Spanish almost never drops *que*.

## quien / quienes — only for people, after a preposition

- *La persona **con quien** hablé.* — The person I spoke with.
- *El amigo **a quien** llamé.* — The friend (whom) I called.

## el que / la que / los que / las que — agreement when clarity matters

When the referent could be ambiguous, use the article-plus-que form so it agrees.

- *La hija del taxista, **la que** vive en Vallarta…* — The taxi driver's daughter, the one who lives in Vallarta…

## cuyo — "whose"

Agrees with the possessed thing.

- *El escritor **cuyas** historias me gustan.* — The writer whose stories I like.

## donde — "where"

For places.

- *El restaurante **donde** comimos ayer.* — The restaurant where we ate yesterday.

## Borges-style sentences

Borges is the master of long sentences chained with *que* and *cuyo*. Reading him is the fastest way to absorb the rhythm of Spanish relative clauses. See [[Borges]].

## Voice note

When you stall mid-sentence, *que* is your bridge. *El lugar... que..., que tiene...* — keep going.
