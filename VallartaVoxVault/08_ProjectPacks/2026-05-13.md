#### Meaning
You missed which gate the agent said. You ask again, politely, without apologizing for existing.

#### Literal
"Disculpe, ¿me-puede repetir el-número de la-puerta?"
Excuse-me, you-can repeat-me the-number of the-gate?

#### Natural
"Disculpe, ¿me puede repetir el número de la puerta?"

#### Grammar Skeleton
- `disculpe` — formal imperative, opens politely without "lo siento" guilt.
- `me puede + infinitive` — the everyday "can you (for me)" construction; clitic `me` rides on the conjugated verb.
- `el número de la puerta` — `de` for possession/relation; `puerta` = gate (and door).

#### Practice Move
Swap `el número de la puerta` for `el nombre del vuelo`, `la hora de salida`, `dónde está el baño`.

#### Saveable Card

- **note:** Polite, no apology. Use `disculpe` to open.

### Borges — El otro, el mismo: Entry Borges La Puerta
*Source: `11_Research/2026-05-13/atelier-entry-borges-la-puerta.md`*

#### Meaning
A single noun carrying weight: not "a" door but **the** door — a particular threshold the speaker already knows.

#### Literal
`la-puerta` — the (feminine) door.

#### Natural
"La puerta" — said with the assumption you already know which one.

#### Grammar Skeleton
The Spanish definite article `la` does heavier work than English "the." It marks the noun as already-known to both speakers — a shared referent. Borges leans on this to make ordinary nouns feel mythic: by saying *la* puerta, he points at a door you and he both already remember.

#### Practice Move
Say `la puerta` aloud, then in your head place a real door behind it (your apartment, the airport gate, the bookshop in Vallarta). Notice how the article changes the door from generic to specific.

#### Saveable Card
- **front:** la puerta
- **back:** the door / the gate / the threshold
- **note:** Definite article in Spanish marks shared knowledge — Borges uses this to mythologize the everyday.
